Starting things off, I wanted to show you how I came to my decision so you can challenge it. First, can a fund mix meet the numerical mandate? Second, would I recommend adopting the full process? At my 9% target, no mix passes, as you can see here. Lowering the target can change the result, but it does not rescue the full process, which I'll demonstrate later on.

I wanted to take you through the repository map and give you a brief summary of what everything does. Starting off from data to verdict, app.py is the interface, portfolio.py does the allocation math, mandate_finder.py tests my fixed weights, auto_pass.py searches for a compatible mix, and process_audit.py reports what blocks adoption. Backtest.py runs the separate timing test. I replaced the six-fund default with ten funds. Prices.csv has 33 common month-end Nasdaq closes. The transform checks dates and positive prices, and the source record preserves checksums. These closes exclude distributions, so I do not treat them as return forecasts.

Going through some of my actual code, I wanted to take you through covariance and portfolio volatilities, along with an overlap-weight code chunk that I wanted to add. The return inputs are editable scenario assumptions. The engine estimates price volatility and covariance, then searches under my return, SGOV reserve, ticker, FBTC, short, gross-exposure, and overlap limits. I can use all 32 monthly returns or the latest 24 for risk. That does not change the assumed returns. I group VOO and VTI's broad U.S. equity exposure, and I do not count TLT as cash. If no mix meets 9%, the model says so rather than forcing a pass.

Jumping into one of the features I added, the mandate finder, here is where I wanted to separate a possible allocation from an adoptable process. The mandate finder shows the limits my chosen weights need. Equal weights support only a 6.45% return target, though FBTC meets its cap. Numerical Auto-Pass keeps the risk limits and finds a 7.75% scenario return, then applies a usable 7.7% target. The full-process audit cannot award a pass while the holdout fails and data remain unresolved.

Jumping into my backtest code, this is the evidence that keeps me from recommending adoption. Backtest.py tests the classroom equity-versus-bills timing rule, I believe from Lab 18. It is not the return of my ten ETFs. I lag the signal by one month and charge 0.1% per switch. Removing the lag creates a look-ahead warning, so I rejected that version. The frozen holdout loses 6.09% net versus a 2.09% buy-and-hold loss. Four development switches imply 400% one-way turnover, well above my 25% limit.

Going down to the app itself and themes, I ran twelve local checks on known answers, data shape, app rendering, boundaries, and verdict separation. I also committed an AI-off prediction before lowering the ticker cap from 40% to 30%. Passing themes at my default were zero. So I lowered the ticker weight to 30% and used numerical Auto-Pass; the only theme that passed was the digital sleeve. [Caption clarification: that on-screen Auto-Pass example is separate from the locked test. The locked 7.5% test changed only the ticker cap from 40% to 30%: passing themes fell from three to one, and the constrained search stayed at 7.55%.]

Peyton's partner run covered my earlier version, and we found issues there that we corrected. These checks and hoops to find a proper portfolio are here because I did not want the committee to have just a cut-and-dry portfolio. I wanted the flexibility to understand that maybe a six-fund portfolio does not work, but through numerical mandates you can find one that does. It gets your mind rolling and gets you more creative.

Overall, this does not supply total-return data, a new partner run, or a passing holdout. My recommendation remains reject and redesign the portfolio.
